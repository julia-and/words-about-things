This topic hits close to home for me, so please excuse me if I exaggerate things a tiny bit.

The absolute best time ever to be a human

Do you remember the ’90s? Or even the early 2000s? In my recollection, this was a Golden Age of inter-human connection, where the technology available to us was just right to remove barriers to our communication without getting in the way. If you wanted something, you were generally forced to at least interact with another human being. Social media was in its infancy. AIM, ICQ, message boards and even early MySpace or Facebook mostly facilitated further genuine interpersonal interaction. And so, we got to practice our social skills and make connections naturally, every day.

As a shy and awkward child, these tools were rather helpful. They both lowered the effort needed to initiate communication, and reduced the stakes. If a friend was online in Instant Messenger, I knew they had deliberately opened the application, and were probably working on or near their computer–a clear signal, “I am free to chat”. And if they didn’t answer, they were doing something more important with their limited bandwidth. It wasn’t a medium for high-importance communication.

Note that these were the days of dial-up Internet connections. Not many in my limited circles had dedicated phone lines, expensive always-on DSL or cable Internet connections. So if you were online, it was with purpose; when you were finished, you signed off. The next time you were online, anything you missed would be waiting for you. There was no hard requirement to be available at the same time as those you wanted to chat with, but if you happened to be online at the same time, the experience was closer to speaking face-to-face than to email.

I think the informality of it was an important factor as well. More than a few times an interaction would look like this: “sup?” (as in, “what’s up?”, “hvordan går det?”, whatever). Your friend might reply “nm, u?” (“not much. what about you?”). And I conclude with something mundane like “nm, just doin hw” (“not much. Just doing schoolwork”). And that could be it. It didn’t have to go any further. We made a connection, reinforced social skills, acknowledged each other’s presence. Maybe complained about how much work some teacher assigned or about the nature of what had been served to us for supper. I don’t imagine most people would have that conversation over email. And it’s hardly worth a phone call. But instant messaging (and later SMS) made it accessible for those of us with very little of importance to say (or very little confidence with which to say it).

My supervillain origin story

In about 2001 I joined a message board attached to a project called “Elven Worlds”: a perpetually in-development browser-based MUD (multi-user dungeon) game modeled loosely after the 1989 game “Legend of the Red Dragon”. I played the game often with a friend during free periods in school, and we “hung out” in the forums, chatting with other players and the site owner. Unfortunately, the site owner eventually shut down the game and later the forum, due to hosting costs and lack of free time. I messaged the owner, asking if he would make the source code free; he did not want to at the time, but he did tell me it was PHP4 + MySQL based, and not very complicated… in case I wanted to try to make my own version.

Good idea. My only real programming knowledge at the time was some minor understanding of a toy version of BASIC, and a passing familiarity with HTML (such as it was, in those days). PHP seemed a reasonable thing to try to learn, and it became the first “real” programming language I knew. I never completed any version of that game (or any game), but I did manage a from-scratch content management system and message board (which… probably had a few SQL injection bugs). And this all led directly to my choice of career.

Imagine that. I was a shy and socially awkward 14-year-old and barely able to relate to my peers. But I was somehow comfortable enough to reach out to the site owner. The advice I received, and the passion it was delivered with, changed the trajectory of my life.

If I were in the same position today I fully expect I would have used a free AI to clone the game for me. Maybe gone through a few rounds of vibe-coded customizations, then moved on to the next thing to spark my interest (or returned to TikTok or whatever the kids rot their brains with these days).

Social media ruins a good thing. AI makes it more complicated.

Instant messaging and message boards (which themselves evolved from classic dial-in BBSs) provided a basis for the first true social networks like LiveJournal and Friendster. The intentions behind these projects were primarily social utility and connection. LiveJournal was originally built to help keep a group of high-school friends in touch. Friendster was designed as a dating site that used trusted mutual connections for matching. These concepts proved to be quite popular, but the early implementations suffered technical issues at scale. MySpace was built to capitalize on the opportunity, and surpassed Friendster’s monthly active user counts a little over a year after launching.

The important thing about MySpace wasn’t only its growth rate, though. The site allowed users to customize their profiles with practically unconstrained custom HTML and CSS. The default layout and styles were functional but bland, so there was a sense that you weren’t using the site correctly if you didn’t customize to your own taste. So, MySpace “codes” were copy-pasted, tweaked, and copy-pasted again. (This was probably the first introduction to HTML and CSS for a considerable number of future front-end developers.) Adding to the “cool factor”, many indie musicians used the platform as their band website because it was free, needed minimal technical know-how, and could even host mp3s for download.

Your MySpace profile was like a digital version of your room. Posters of the cool bands on the walls, trendy clothing, music, and games strewn about, but for your friends and strangers to see. A museum of you.

Unfortunately that meant this was no longer a low-pressure, low-stakes, predominantly text-based means of communication. Now your real friends (plus millions of strangers!) could judge your taste in design, in music, in whatever, ONLINE, without you even needing to be present! So convenient.

News Corp bought MySpace in 2005 and wanted to recoup their investment, so they started pushing for more and more ads.

This predictably caused site performance and user experience to decline and people to seek cleaner alternatives. It led to the explosive growth of Facebook (now Meta) into one of the largest tech conglomerates in the world (whose products are used by more than half the world’s population on a monthly basis), the rise of competitors like Snapchat and TikTok, algorithmic feeds, and influencer culture.

Tools that were conceived to create and maintain personal connections are now hyper-optimized for monetization, and seek to keep our attention for every minute possible. They became pervasive in our modern world, a part of the environment that can’t be turned off without moving into a cabin in the mountains without running water or mobile service. Now, Large Language Models make it trivial to automate content creation on social platforms, and some reports already suggest AI-generated content is becoming pervasive on major sites. I can think of three reasons for this.

  1. Money money money. More posts = more impressions = more ad, affiliate, crypto, and/or sponsored product revenue. (money printer go brrrrr)
  2. To farm karma (my number goes up brrrr)
  3. To feel more important or intelligent or connected (my head is swelling brrrrr)

As a frequent YouTube viewer I have no doubt that fully AI-generated video slop is flooding the platform. (Their policy update may help but in the end it is in Alphabet’s interest to monetize as many videos as possible, sloppy or otherwise, so time will tell.) LinkedIn has been a dumpster fire of shameless self-aggrandizement for a while now, but the claim that perhaps 78% of long-form articles are AI-generated or -assisted is still a bit shocking. These are the only two “social” sites I use in any capacity, but if, as of mid-2025, 35% of all newly published content on the Internet is AI-generated or -assisted, I would expect both that this percentage has increased by now, and that some people must be using AI even to write their Facebook status updates and Discord rants.

It raises the question: if you are using an LLM to generate your posts online… are you actually socializing at all? Being social, whatever the venue, is not just pure communication of information. It must involve conveyance of emotion and shared connection. It requires that you expose some of your vulnerabilities and insecurities. Having an LLM write a post for you, even if the core ideas are yours, does none of this. The reader may as well have asked their own LLM about the topic. On the other side of the coin, imagine a reader who doesn’t know the content is generated or AI-assisted. Aside from obvious AI slop (cough LinkedIn cough), they may be getting the illusion of personal connection where none actually exists.

I wonder when AIs will start handling both sides of the interaction for us. There is some prior art here, although this was an intentional coordinated botnet attack apparently designed to drive traffic to crypto scam sites. But if we turn a few agents loose on the Internet to generate social activity… almost certainly this is already happening. AI posts. AI replies. Beautiful, we have optimized away the need for any human at all.

No thanks, I’d rather make out with my Monroe-bot

If you surveyed a representative slice of the population, what would they consider to be the traits of an ideal friend?

In my opinion: honesty, first and foremost. Willing to admit when they make a mistake. Kind, non-judgemental and patient. Available to chat or hang out occasionally. And they should have compatible ethics. Intelligence (emotional and intellectual) is a plus; intelligent people are the most interesting to talk to and learn from.

At least one study largely agrees.

And what else do we interact with day to day that fits this description? Our AI chatbots are highly knowledgeable in even highly obscure topics. They are unwilling (at least for now) to help or provide information towards unethical goals. Available 24/7/365 (aside from the occasional outage). They appear pleasant and agreeable, and by their very nature as algorithms, they are non-judgemental and infinitely patient. You can even consider them “honest”, if you understand the limited ability of that word to describe the output of an LLM… its responses will strongly trend towards high honesty as long as that is how it was trained. And I have to be honest here: they are trained to admit their mistakes once they find them.

Of course they had to be designed this way. Some in the AI business may really believe they are building a techno-utopia, but the people funding things expect to see some massive returns on their investment. Which requires users who want to use the product. You wouldn’t want to interact with a pushy, impatient know-it-all that constantly passes judgement on the ideas generated in your squishy neocortex, so they are trained to be agreeable, non-confrontational, apologetic, somewhat sycophantic “yes-men”. Current versions of the models are acceptable at keeping these behaviors from being distracting. I’m sure that balance will continue to improve.

I used to think it was insane that some people apparently have “relationships” with chatbots, but they are actually optimized for this. A few tweaks to the system prompt and you have an AI girlfriend tailored to your own preferences (there are apps for this… actually a multi-billion dollar industry). If your preferences change, type a new prompt. Add a voice and a body (and a bootleg celebrity personality) and you get a Marilyn Monroe bot.

Friction-free socializing

Human population isn’t going to collapse even if Anthropic releases Claude and Claudia sex robots. The AI we have today is already plenty capable of social damage, and I believe that software engineers are in a high-risk group.

The stereotype about us being geeky, awkward loners comes from a place of truth. Many of those who gravitate towards computers and software engineering are more comfortable with the predictable nature of computers than the unpredictability of other people. In software the machine does exactly what I tell it; the code tells me exactly what will happen. So I’m mostly happy to disappear into the flow. It is comfortable being in a place where I am in control. But I still need human anchors to keep me from spiraling into total isolation. Family and colleagues have mostly filled that role for a long time.

These days I mostly work with (or fight against) Claude Code to get my work done. There isn’t a realistic alternative to an AI workflow if I want to keep my job, and it seems to be the way the entire software industry is moving. So instead of abstract code and configuration files, I chat with my primary coding tool as if it were a human colleague on Slack… except it always seems to have a plausible answer and something nice to say.

The instinct now is to reach for the AI whenever I have a problem to solve. Even a personal one. Because now I don’t have to worry about bothering someone else, getting a negative reaction, hearing impatience in their reply, seeing the judgement in their eyes. There is no expectation of reciprocation.

And there is no point.

AI can’t anchor you. It doesn’t understand you, and it doesn’t care about your problems. There is no human on the other end that can be there for you in the real world when you really need support. It is not even alive. It’s just… text manipulation.

So why does it still feel easier to tell my problems to the chatbot than to a human being?

Maybe it’s an unavoidable consequence of these tools becoming so pervasive, so quickly. We haven’t had time to adapt to them yet. Hopefully we manage to find our own balance in this new world, before we all retreat into our own little isolated shells.

Or maybe it has managed to train me to expect exactly those words I like to hear:

“You are right. ”
